# Posting cadence

How often, when, and in what order — decided once so a month does not clump.

## Sustainable frequency

| Platform | Sustainable | Ambitious | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | 2–3/week | 5/week | Long-form; each post is real work |
| X | 4–7/week | 2–3/day | Short; the only platform where daily is routine |
| Instagram | 2–3/week | 5/week | Needs an image every time — the real constraint |
| Facebook | 2–3/week | 5/week | Low ceiling for most B2B |
| TikTok | 2–3/week | daily | Needs video; rarely a fit for a text-first workflow |

**Choose the cadence the brand can hold**, not the one that looks impressive.
Daily LinkedIn from a team of one is a plan that fails in week two, and a feed
that stops abruptly reads worse than one that was always modest.

Say the assumptions out loud — team size, whether video is available, weekdays
only — because a user can only correct an assumption they can see.

## Timing

Treat times as **defaults, not claims**. Real optimal times vary by audience
and are only knowable from a brand's own data, which plgn does not hold.

- **B2B** — weekday mornings, in the audience's timezone.
- **B2C** — evenings and weekends skew better.
- **Avoid** Friday afternoons and public holidays in the audience's region.

Never claim an engagement lift from a send time. Say "a reasonable default"
and move on.

## Spacing rules

- **No pillar clumps.** One pillar must not own a whole week. Rotate so
  consecutive posts come from different pillars.
- **Vary hook shapes** across consecutive slots, per **platform-specs**. Two
  number-led hooks back to back read as a template even when the content
  differs.
- **Space the same platform evenly.** Three LinkedIn posts should be Mon /
  Wed / Fri, not three in one afternoon.
- **Leave the last week lighter.** Something always displaces it — a launch, a
  holiday, an emergency. A month with no slack is a month that breaks.
- **Never schedule two posts to the same platform within a few hours**, unless
  the platform is X and the brand genuinely posts that way.

## Fewer good posts than slots

This happens constantly, and how it is handled separates a useful schedule from
a damaging one.

**Thin the schedule. Do not pad it.**

A padded post is not neutral — it reaches real followers, teaches them the
brand's posts are skippable, and makes the next good post less likely to be
read. The cost is paid on the *following* post, which is why padding feels free
and is not.

When it happens:

1. Schedule the strong posts.
2. Reduce the cadence to match what exists.
3. **Say so**, with the reason:

> Scheduled 22 of the planned 28. Two pillars supported fewer posts than
> planned — padding them would have meant publishing filler.

The plan already told the user how many to expect, so a shortfall is
information, not a failure. Never quietly generate weak posts to hit a number.

## Reducing an over-ambitious cadence

If a requested cadence cannot be sustained, say so **before** scheduling, not
after:

> 5 LinkedIn posts a week needs roughly 5 hours of writing. At 3/week the
> quality holds and the pillars last twice as long. Which do you want?

Give the user the trade-off and let them choose. Do not silently reduce, and do
not silently comply with a cadence that will produce filler.
